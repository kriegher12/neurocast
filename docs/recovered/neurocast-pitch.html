Major project proposal · speaker script

What are brain decoders really reading?

Script for presenting NeuroCast to your faculty guide. Read the serif text aloud; the amber lines say what to show. Numbers are from the project README §4 (real LibriBrain100 data, 32 listeners, as of 29 September 2026). Replace the [bracketed] parts before you present.

If you only get 30 seconds

We audit AI systems that claim to read words from brain signals. On the main public MEG benchmark, we found that the timing of speech alone, with no brain data, predicts the heard word better than published brain decoders do. We built a test that separates timing from genuine brain information: about half of a decoder's score survives it, and stronger decoders find more. Next, we want to use that test to compare how brain AI models should be pre-trained.

Opening

Good morning. We are [names]. Our proposed major project is called NeuroCast.

The question behind it is simple. When an AI system claims to read words from brain signals, what is it actually reading: the word, the person, or something else?

We have already built the software and run the first experiments on real brain recordings from 32 people. Today we would like to show you what we found, what we plan to do next, and ask for your feedback on the scope.

MEG and the benchmark

0:45 → 2:15
Say

Some background. MEG, magnetoencephalography, measures the tiny magnetic fields produced by brain activity. The scanner is a helmet with 306 sensors. In our data it records 250 times a second, and it needs no surgery.

A major goal in the field is decoding speech from brain signals, for example to help people who cannot speak. The main public benchmark today is LibriBrain100, from Oxford, and the 2026 PNPL competition is built on it. People listen to Sherlock Holmes audiobooks inside the scanner. The task: given half a second of brain signal starting when a word was heard, say which of 50 common words it was.

Scores are balanced accuracy: every word counts equally. With ten guesses, random guessing gets 20 percent. The best published system gets 43 percent on new people, and 73 percent on one person with 80 hours of recordings. The competition itself scores the single best guess, where chance is 2 percent.

The problem

2:15 → 3:45
Say

A high score does not prove that a model is reading the word. Three recent results made us worried.

First, an audit of EEG AI models this year found that their internal features mostly encode who the person is, 13 to 89 times more than chance. Second, another 2026 study showed a decoder scoring well on pure noise under a careless test setup. Third, a September 2026 roadmap for MEG foundation models says the most basic comparison, which way of pre-training works best at equal data and compute, has never been done.

So we asked two questions. What are MEG word decoders actually reading? And does training a model to predict the future of the brain signal, instead of filling in blanks, give better representations that encode less about the person?

Our idea

3:45 → 5:15
Say

The project has two parts.

Part one is an audit. Every number we report comes with its controls: a measured chance level, fake signals that keep some properties of the real one but remove the information, and a check of how much of a model's features is about the person rather than the word.

Part two is NeuroCast itself: a transformer model for MEG that works with any sensor layout and can be trained with seven different objectives. The two that matter most are forecasting, "predict what comes next" the way GPT learns from text, and masked reconstruction, "fill in the blanks", which is what current MEG models use.

Our original plan was to compare those fairly. In practice the audit produced the most important results so far, so we propose to centre the project on the audit and keep the pre-training comparison as the second stage.

What we found

6:45 → 10:15 · the core of the talk, rehearse this part most
ShowThe score chart below while you talk through findings one and two.
Say

First finding: timing. A window that starts at a word also contains the brain's response to the next words, and the next word arrives sooner after short words like "a" or "the". So we built a classifier that never sees brain data, only when the words around a word start. It gets the word into its top ten 61 to 67 percent of the time, and its single guess is right 15 to 18 percent of the time, against 2 percent chance. That is higher than the best published brain decoder on new people. In the competition's test data, word onsets within each sentence are provided, so this information is available to everyone.

Second: our brain decoders. On all 32 people, tested on a different chapter of the book, a linear decoder scores 0.257 with ten guesses, a small neural network 0.278, and a CNN on the raw signal 0.298. Chance is 0.20. We then built a control that feeds the decoder a fake signal carrying only word timing. It reproduces between a quarter and a half of the linear decoder's gain.

Third: the decisive test. We matched trials on speech timing, word length and word loudness, measured from the actual audiobook audio, and asked whether the brain still helps. Before running it we fixed the rule: if less than a third of the gain survives, the decoder is mostly reading timing. About half survives. And the stronger the decoder, the more genuine word information it finds: the CNN finds five times as much as the linear decoder. So the brain signal is real, but small, and published scores cannot yet say how much of their number is timing.

Fourth: pre-training. In a pilot on our GPU, we found that the masked model, the approach current systems use, had collapsed on real data: its output ignored its input. We diagnosed why, fixed it, and ran a fair comparison. The two approaches decode about equally well, but the forecasting model packs about three times more information about who the person is into its features. That is the opposite of what we expected, and it is exactly why this comparison needs to be done properly.

One more measurement from the project: we asked how predictable the listening brain is. Almost all of it comes from the signal's frequency spectrum, and about half of what first looked like extra structure was the recording changing between sessions.

The ML setup, spoken version

10:15 → 12:45 · full details in the reference section below
Say

Briefly, the technical setup.

Data. 306 channels at 250 Hz. Each trial is a window of 128 samples, half a second, starting at a word, for the 50 most frequent words. We fit on one session and test on a different chapter of the book, so no audio is shared between training and testing: about 32,000 trials each. We report balanced accuracy with one guess, which the competition scores, and with ten.

Decoders. The linear decoder averages each channel over 64-millisecond patches, keeps 128 principal components learned from training data only, and uses ridge regression. The neural network adds two hidden layers of 256 units. The CNN works on the raw signal at 125 Hz: one layer mixes the 306 sensors into 32, two temporal convolution layers follow, then pooling and a linear output. It trains in about 20 seconds on our GPU.

NeuroCast. Each sensor is described by its position, orientation and type, so any MEG or EEG layout works without new weights. The signal is cut into 64-millisecond patches; a perceiver compresses the sensors into 5 region tokens per patch; a transformer with rotary position encoding processes the sequence, one-directional for forecasting and two-way for masked reconstruction. We have run sizes from 1.3 to 25 million parameters; the design goes up to 320 million. Training uses AdamW with a learning rate of 10⁻³, warm-up and cosine decay, gradient clipping and mixed precision, and compute is measured in floating-point operations so every objective gets exactly the same budget.

What we plan to add. MEG-XL, the current state-of-the-art model, put through the same controls on university GPUs. NeuroCast at 25 million parameters, and larger if compute allows, trained on 5 to 20 hours of data with three random seeds, comparing forecasting against the fixed masked model. And a second dataset, MEG-MASC, to check that the timing effect is not specific to LibriBrain.

Data and protocol

DatasetLibriBrain100 (Oxford PNPL), public, CC-BY-NC-4.0 (non-commercial). 32 "broad" listeners: sub-1…12 whole sessions (~24 min each), sub-13…22 first halves, sub-23…32 first quarters. Deep listener sub-0: ~80 h (3 sessions, 47.5 min, used for pre-training pilots).
Signal306 MEG channels (102 magnetometers, 204 planar gradiometers, MEGIN), preprocessed, 250 Hz. Converted to fixed "canonical" units per sensor type.
Task50-word classification at word onsets: 50 most frequent words, or the competition's official list. Window 0.512 s (128 samples) from onset; 1.0 s (the competition's window) also tested.
SplitFit on session 11 (chapter 11), test on session 12 (chapter 12): stimulus-disjoint. 32,198 fit and 32,251 test trials. Split checks run automatically; windows are fixed length.
NormalisationFixed units ("type-const"); per-session median removal with one scale per sensor family ("session-scalar", used for the headline results); per-channel robust scaling ("session-robust").
MetricsBalanced accuracy top-1 (competition metric, chance 0.02) and top-10 (paper's headline, chance 0.20). Held-out log-likelihood in nats for the information tests.

Baselines that never see the brain

Timing classifierClass-balanced multinomial logistic regression (L-BFGS) on timing features binned into 12 quantiles plus raw values: onsets of the next four words inside the window, gap since the previous word, position in sentence. Trained on 60,000 words from the deep listener's other sessions.
Language modelWord bigram, scored as pointwise mutual information (so word frequency earns nothing under balanced accuracy); trained on 650,000 words from 112 training sessions. Uses the true previous word, so it measures headroom, not a deployable decoder.

Controls and tests on every result

  • Shuffled-label null: the decoder refitted 10–20 times on shuffled labels.
  • Fake signals: window mean only; Gaussian noise with the right mean and variance; phase-scrambled (keeps the power spectrum, destroys timing); onset-only (the recording's average word-onset response placed at the true onsets, plus another trial's remainder as noise).
  • Identity audit: share of feature variance explained by the person, against a person-shuffled null; nearest-centroid person probe.
  • Timing-matched test: strata by next-word onset (50 ms bins), gap since previous word, second onset in window, and duration and loudness tertiles; the decoder chooses only among words present in a stratum; each scorer gets its own permutation null (shuffled by word occurrence).
  • Information beyond covariates: held-out log-likelihood gain over a no-brain model of timing, duration and loudness; every word weighted equally; cross-fitted by word occurrence; cluster-bootstrap intervals. Verified to give zero for scores that carry only word frequency.

Questions to expect

Suggested answers, in your own words
Timing is part of speech. Why call it a confound?

For a word-identity benchmark, the claim is that the model recognises which word was heard. The timing of the next words tells you the word's length and context without any brain data, so it has to be separated out. We do not say timing is useless; we measure how much of a score it explains and how much is word-specific brain information.

Your numbers are lower than the published 43%. Why?

Our decoders are small and trained in seconds; MEG-XL is a pre-trained 20M-parameter model. The evaluation windows also differ (the paper uses 0.2–0.6 s after onset). Our goal is to measure what scores mean, not to beat the state of the art; putting MEG-XL through our controls is in the plan.

How do you know there is no leakage?

Training and test use different chapters of the book; windows are fixed length; split checks run automatically; every score has a shuffled-label null; Gaussian noise stays at chance; permutations respect that all listeners heard the same words; and each part is validated against synthetic data with known answers.

What is new here?

A no-brain timing baseline and a timing-matched evaluation for LibriBrain word decoding; an identity audit of MEG models (so far only EEG models have been audited); a fair forecasting-versus-masked comparison, which the 2026 roadmap says is missing; and a diagnosed collapse of the masked objective on real MEG. We did not find these in the literature we checked, and we will search more thoroughly before claiming them.

Isn't the masked collapse just a bug in your code?

It is a training failure, not a wiring error: gradients do flow, and an untrained model passes its input through. We showed the information is there (a linear predictor recovers 34–48% of a hidden patch from its neighbours), that lower learning rates and other targets still collapse, and that also scoring the visible positions fixes it. Our diagnostic script can check any masked checkpoint for the same failure.

What if forecasting does not beat masking?

Either answer is informative, and the pilot already shows an unexpected direction on identity. The audit results stand on their own.

How much compute do you need?

Everything so far ran on one laptop. The next stage needs roughly 100–200 GPU-hours (an estimate), most of it for MEG-XL, which needs an 80 GB GPU to fine-tune. The pre-training comparison at 25–63M parameters is cheap by comparison.

Ethics and data use?

LibriBrain is public and anonymised, licensed for non-commercial use, which covers academic work. Brain recordings are biometric: our identity results show why models should be checked for it. One possible issue with the competition data we will report privately to the organisers rather than publish.

How was the code written? Who did what?

Answer honestly and follow your college's rules on reused code and AI tools. If you used an AI coding assistant, say so, explain that every part is checked by the validation suite, and name which modules each member owns and can explain. Ask the guide how they want this documented in the report.

What do "BAcc@10" and "nats" mean?

BAcc@10: whether the right word is among the top ten guesses, averaged over the 50 words so rare words count as much as common ones; chance is 20%. Nats: a unit of information using the natural logarithm. Naming one of 50 words takes about 3.9 nats; our best decoder adds about 0.01 nats per word beyond timing, length and loudness.
